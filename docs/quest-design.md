# Quest and narrative design: the 12 rules and the five questions

Jerry, 2026-10-24, after his 1.0 playthrough. Every quest, day mission, side story and piece of the main story follows
this. Any agent who designs or changes one answers the five questions (below) in its spec before building it
(AGENTS.md). His words, lightly tidied.

The secret to a compelling interconnected game narrative is to make the main story feel like the world's central
conflict, while side quests feel like the countless lives, mysteries, and consequences unfolding because of it. The
player should never feel like they are abandoning the story to complete unrelated errands. Instead, every adventure
should deepen their understanding of the world, reveal something unexpected, or influence what happens next.

## The 12 rules

1. **The Narrative Spine.** Establish one overarching mystery, conflict, or threat that drives the entire game. Every
   major storyline should connect to this central conflict either directly, indirectly, or thematically. The main story
   provides direction; side quests provide depth.
2. **Cause and Effect.** Quests should emerge from events occurring in the world rather than appearing as isolated
   assignments. A supernatural outbreak might cause military deployments, civilian disappearances, abandoned
   settlements, strange wildlife behavior, and competing investigations. Each becomes a potential quest with a shared
   underlying cause.
3. **Independent Stories.** Every side quest should tell a satisfying miniature story with its own beginning,
   escalation, and resolution. Its value should not depend entirely on its connection to the main quest. A
   missing-person investigation should be compelling even before the player discovers its significance to the larger
   narrative.
4. **Overlapping Threads.** Introduce characters, locations, organizations, and mysteries in multiple storylines. A
   scientist rescued during an optional mission might later appear at a military research facility. A seemingly
   insignificant artifact might resurface in a completely different investigation. These connections make the world
   feel interconnected rather than artificially assembled.
5. **Unequal Knowledge.** Different characters and factions should possess different pieces of the truth. Soldiers
   understand the tactical situation, villagers remember ancient legends, researchers recognize anomalous phenomena, and
   hostile organizations conceal critical information. Exploring multiple questlines allows players to assemble a
   larger picture that no individual character fully understands.
6. **Escalating Mystery.** Answers should occasionally introduce more fascinating questions. Discovering what caused a
   village's disappearance might reveal that the phenomenon has occurred for centuries. Eventually, apparently unrelated
   incidents should begin forming a recognizable pattern. Mystery creates curiosity; carefully timed revelations reward
   it.
7. **Persistent Consequences.** Completed quests should leave evidence in the world. Rescued civilians return home,
   abandoned outposts become operational, characters remember the player's decisions, and unresolved supernatural
   threats continue affecting their surroundings. The world should acknowledge what happened, even when the
   consequences are small.
8. **Thematic Consistency.** Individual stories can vary dramatically in tone while still exploring the same
   underlying ideas. Horror, humor, tragedy, military action, and quiet exploration can coexist if they revolve around
   recurring themes such as humanity confronting the unknown, the cost of survival, or the conflict between scientific
   understanding and ancient belief.
9. **Narrative Breathing Room.** Alternate intense missions with quieter experiences. A terrifying underground
   operation might be followed by an investigation in a peaceful mountain village, an unusual conversation with a local
   resident, or the restoration of an abandoned military station. Contrast prevents emotional exhaustion and makes
   moments of horror or wonder more memorable.
10. **Meaningful Optionality.** Side quests should enrich the player's experience without being mandatory to understand
    the central story. Players who follow only the main campaign receive a complete narrative. Players who explore
    thoroughly discover deeper explanations, alternative solutions, additional allies, and consequences that reward
    their curiosity.
11. **Converging Storylines.** Allow separate questlines to intersect naturally at important moments. An ancient relic,
    missing military patrol, and strange radio transmission might initially appear unrelated but eventually converge
    around a single supernatural event. The player should occasionally experience the satisfaction of realizing that
    several mysteries were connected all along.
12. **Earned Payoff.** Important details introduced early should occasionally acquire greater significance later. A
    strange children's rhyme, an unexplained symbol, or a malfunctioning radio can seem like atmospheric background
    detail until a later revelation transforms its meaning. The strongest twists make players reinterpret things they
    have already experienced.

## The five questions (every quest answers them in its spec)

- **Why does this quest exist?** What event, character, or environmental condition caused it?
- **Why should the player care?** What mystery, emotional stake, gameplay challenge, or potential reward makes it
  interesting?
- **How does it connect?** Which faction, place, character, event, theme, or mystery ties it to the wider world?
- **What changes afterward?** How does completing, failing, or ignoring the quest affect the world or the player's
  understanding?
- **What makes it memorable?** What unique discovery, encounter, decision, visual moment, or narrative reversal
  distinguishes it from other quests?

A quest that cannot satisfactorily answer these questions probably needs to be redesigned.

## One final principle

Do not connect absolutely everything. Some mysteries should remain local, some characters should have problems
unrelated to the main conflict, and some discoveries should never receive complete explanations. A world in which
everything secretly revolves around one villain or central mystery eventually feels artificial.

The ultimate objective is to create the feeling that the player is exploring a living world filled with stories, rather
than progressing through a collection of missions written specifically for them. The main narrative gives that
exploration purpose, while the side stories make the world worth caring about.
