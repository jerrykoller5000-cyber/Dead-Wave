# World design: the sweeps, the 15 rules, the look

Jerry, 2026-10-24, after his 1.0 playthrough. This is how the world is placed, checked and judged from now on. Every
agent who places, moves, scales or builds anything in the world reads it first (AGENTS.md). His words, lightly
tidied; the crew's working checklist is at the end.

## The two sweeps

"We are going to do what I call a sweep of all the placed items in the world. I want you guys to go through and look
at absolutely everything and make and check and correct these principles."

### The placement sweep

1. Are any objects blocking another object?
2. Do the distances between objects or locations make sense visually and aesthetically?
3. Does the scale of the objects make sense?

### The transition sweep

1. Do the objects transition in a way that makes sense?
2. If an object were to be in contact with another, what would that look like?
3. Are there any problems visually or aesthetically in between objects that do not make any sense or are broken?
   - A. Is anything floating that should not be?
   - B. Is anything clipping that should not be?
   - C. Do the objects look visually appealing and accurate?

## The 15 rules of spatial world design

1. **Logical Proximity.** Objects that serve related purposes should exist near one another. A woodpile belongs near a
   fireplace or chopping block, tools belong near workstations, and vegetation should reflect nearby water, sunlight,
   and terrain.
2. **Environmental Causality.** Every object's position should have an understandable cause. A fallen tree may block a
   trail, rocks accumulate beneath cliffs, and abandoned carts appear along roads rather than in arbitrary locations.
3. **Clustering.** Natural environments favor irregular clusters over uniform distributions. Trees grow in groves,
   rocks form outcroppings, and settlements gather around resources. Avoid evenly spaced objects unless the environment
   was deliberately organized.
4. **Negative Space.** Empty space is as important as occupied space. Clearings make forests feel dense, empty plazas
   emphasize surrounding architecture, and open ground creates contrast with cluttered environments.
5. **Visual Hierarchy.** Larger, distinctive, or more important objects should establish the structure of an area.
   Smaller objects should reinforce those anchors rather than compete with them. A massive ancient tree might dominate a
   clearing, with roots, stones, plants, and fallen branches arranged around it.
6. **Spatial Rhythm.** Alternate between density and openness, enclosure and exposure, simplicity and complexity.
   Constant visual density becomes exhausting and makes environments feel repetitive.
7. **Partial Revelation.** Important locations and objects should sometimes be partially obscured by terrain,
   vegetation, architecture, or atmospheric effects. Allow players to discover things gradually rather than seeing
   everything immediately.
8. **Functional Accessibility.** Objects designed for interaction must have appropriate surrounding space. Doors need
   clearance, workstations need standing areas, pathways must accommodate movement, and climbable surfaces must be
   reachable.
9. **Scale Relationships.** Object sizes and distances should communicate believable proportions. Furniture should
   relate to human dimensions, buildings should accommodate their intended occupants, and natural features should
   maintain convincing geological relationships.
10. **Controlled Irregularity.** Nature rarely produces perfect repetition. Vary orientation, spacing, height, and
    grouping, but preserve underlying environmental logic. Randomness should modify a believable arrangement, not
    determine its entire structure.
11. **Directional Guidance.** Object placement should subtly influence player movement. Roads, fences, lights,
    architecture, tree lines, and natural openings can direct attention toward interesting locations without explicit
    markers.
12. **Spatial Storytelling.** Groups of objects should imply past events. A broken wagon surrounded by scattered
    supplies suggests an accident or attack. An abandoned campsite with a recently extinguished fire suggests someone
    departed in a hurry.
13. **Ecological and Structural Dependence.** Objects must respect their environment and supporting structures. Plants
    need suitable growing conditions, buildings require plausible foundations, and heavy objects need surfaces capable
    of supporting them.
14. **Meaningful Contrast.** Place unusual objects against familiar surroundings to generate interest. A solitary
    illuminated window in an abandoned village, an ancient statue deep in an ordinary forest, or a pristine doorway in a
    ruined structure naturally attracts attention.
15. **Rewarded Curiosity.** The arrangement of objects should encourage exploration and occasionally reward
    observation. A narrow gap between rocks, an unusual cluster of trees, or an isolated staircase should sometimes lead
    to something worth discovering, rather than always being meaningless decoration.

### Example: building a forest clearing

Imagine a cabin in a forest. Instead of placing objects independently, construct relationships.

- The cabin occupies relatively level, accessible ground.
- A worn footpath connects its doorway to a nearby stream.
- A woodpile rests under the roof overhang, protected from rain.
- Mushrooms and moss gather in shaded, moist areas.
- Large trees surround the clearing, while smaller plants populate its edges.
- A fallen tree interrupts part of the path, suggesting an alternative route.
- A faint lantern glow beyond the tree line invites investigation.

Each object helps explain the placement of the others. The result feels like a location that existed before the player
arrived.

### The placement hierarchy

1. **Primary anchors:** landmarks, terrain formations, major structures.
2. **Connections:** paths, roads, waterways, sightlines.
3. **Supporting objects:** trees, furniture, ruins, equipment.
4. **Micro-details:** debris, footprints, vegetation, wear, storytelling clues.

Place large-scale anchors first, then establish how they connect, then populate the supporting environment, and finally
add small details. Validate movement and sightlines throughout the process.

**The overarching rule:** a great environment should not look like someone placed a collection of assets into empty
space. It should look like the environment itself determined where those assets belonged. For an exploration-focused
game, object placement should create curiosity, anticipation, discovery, and surprise. The relationship between objects
should not only make the world believable; it should make players want to see what lies beyond the next hill.

## The look: visual aesthetics and fidelity

The players are going to spend a lot of time in the main world. The more immersive and personality the world has, the
more the player will enjoy returning time and time again. Think of worlds like Skyrim and Minecraft, the worlds that
have made millions of players homesick. The magic of an unforgettable game lies not in the amount of content it offers,
but in its ability to make players feel as though they have stepped into a living, breathing world that exists beyond
their presence. Every distant landmark should inspire curiosity, every forgotten corner should hold the possibility of
discovery, and every journey should feel meaningful even without a destination. The world must possess a history that
can be felt rather than simply explained, with environments, music, atmosphere, and subtle storytelling working together
to evoke wonder, mystery, solitude, and belonging. Freedom should encourage players to follow their instincts rather
than objectives, allowing unexpected experiences to become their most treasured memories. Above all, the world should
feel like a place rather than a product: somewhere players don't merely visit to complete tasks, but return to simply
because they miss being there.

Visually: worn military realism collides with eerie folklore and decaying industrial mystery.

- **Places:** pine-dark forests, foggy mountain passes, and wind-beaten villages lit by sodium lamps and dying
  fluorescent bulbs; old stone ruins and weathered timber structures beside brutalist research bunkers, radar sites, and
  improvised forward operating bases.
- **Palette:** cold blue-grays, mossy greens, rust, amber lamp glow, and faded institutional yellows: a constant tension
  between cozy and unsettling.
- **Interiors:** tactile and slightly off. Stitched fabrics, chipped enamel, analog dials, children's drawings pinned
  beside threat maps, strange relics locked in evidence cages, gas masks hanging beside prayer charms. Everything looks
  familiar, but just wrong enough to suggest something supernatural is pressing in from the edges.
- **The uncanny:** creatures, anomalies, and haunted spaces are not flashy or overly magical. They feel quiet, physical,
  and invasive, as if the mythic has seeped into a world of rifles, concrete, and field manuals.
- **Tone:** equal parts melancholic, uncanny, ancient, and lived-in.

## The crew's working checklist (Claude, for the sweeps)

Every location gets one pass, in the hierarchy's order: its anchor, then the connections to it, then the supporting
props, then the details. For each:

- **The numbers** (tools/sweep.mjs, the test page): every placed object's box, the ground under its footprint, what it
  overlaps, how far it floats or sinks, its size against the marine (1.8 m), what's within 2 m of every door, panel, ladder
  and prompt. The tool lists suspects; it doesn't decide.
- **The eyes** (page shots, the real renderer): the location from the approach path, from the marine's height in first
  person, from above, and at night. Antigravity shoots on Jerry's GPU; the cloud shoots in software.
- **The questions:** the six sweep questions above, then the 15 rules, then the look. A finding names the rule it breaks.
- **The fix** goes where the object is placed (its owner's file), with the before and after shot. Placement and scale
  are fixed in the sweep; a prop that needs a new model is a request to its owner (Antigravity for props and buildables).
- **Nothing in the way of play:** a move must not change a path the dead walk, a test's coordinates or a mission's
  reach without its test changed in the same task.
