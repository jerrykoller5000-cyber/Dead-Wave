## Side hops: make the legs propel the body

`side-hop-cycle-detail.png` shows consecutive frames from 0.4–1.5 seconds. At 0.5–0.7 the dancer is low with knees/hips compressed and arms down; at 0.8 the legs extend and arms drive upward. Around 0.9–1.1 both feet gather underneath the hips with visible knee flexion. At 1.2–1.3 the legs lengthen toward the destination. At 1.4–1.5 the landing is absorbed through the knees and hips while the arms lower. The loop starts mid-flight, so frame zero is not a neutral starting pose.

The important differences from a rigid translated figure are joint compression, a real push, the knees folding in flight, and landing before reversing direction. The torso leans in response to the jump while the head stays more level; the arms are asymmetric and loose, not rigid wings.

Suggested adaptation to a single musical beat (these percentages are proposed, not measured):

1. 0–18%: feet anchored; compress hips/knees and swing arms down/back.
2. 18–30%: push upward and sideways by extending the legs, then leave the floor.
3. 30–72%: travel laterally on an arc; shorten the hip-to-boot distance so knees tuck. Raise arms in a soft V, then begin lowering them.
4. 72–100%: land with extended feet, then flex knees/hips to absorb momentum. Keep landing feet anchored before the next launch.

Do not slide the whole rig sideways during the planted phases. Flight foot targets should approach the hips rather than remain at a fixed ground-relative position. The visible tuck can be modest; the sequence of compression, extension, tuck, extension, absorption matters more than exaggeration.

## Standing hip-hop: relaxed asymmetry and weight transfer

`dance-hiphop-sheet.png` shows alternating support legs and small heel/toe pivots. The planted leg receives the hip weight while the free foot sweeps or lifts. Hands mostly stay near waist/chest height, elbows remain comfortably bent, and shoulder/hip motion follows the weight transfer with a slight lag. The movement is casual and unequal from side to side. The turn around 6.7–7.8 seconds develops through steps and pivots instead of an instant full-body yaw.

Use this for the opening groove: smaller arm sweeps, clearer one-leg support, uneven shoulder accents, and a short delay between hip travel and chest/head response. Continuous big knee lifts and mechanically mirrored arm pumping would lose the reference's feel.

## Floor spin: leg scissors create the motion

`break-dance-sheet.png` shows repeated wide scissoring legs and rolling support across the shoulders/upper back. One leg rises while the other sweeps sideways; the pelvis is not a fixed horizontal platter. Hands briefly help the transitions, and the upper body changes its floor contact as it rotates. Around 3.0–3.3 seconds the dancer reaches toward the floor and rotates the chest downward; by 3.4–3.5 the spin resolves into a low forearm/palm-supported freeze, with the legs still asymmetric.

For the game, make the long leg lead the rotation and let the pelvis roll across the support side. Decelerate by opening the shape and placing the supporting arm before the final freeze. Avoid a spinning upright skeleton simply rotated onto its back, and avoid standing up between the spin and finale.

## Comedic spin-to-freeze finale

`old-man-in-blue-break-dance-gglhzvd74jz89flm-sheet.png` gives the clearest ending. The initial bent-leg lunge drops onto the side/back at 0–0.75 seconds. From roughly 1.0–2.5 the legs pull toward the torso as the body turns. The silhouette opens at 2.75–3.0, then settles at 3.25 into a relaxed side recline: one elbow on the floor with that hand at the cheek, the other palm grounded, one leg extended, and the other bent forward. The last three frames hold essentially the same pose through 4.0 seconds.

This is a strong finale for the boastful character: finish the last rotation facing the audience, lower onto the supporting hip/elbow, let the spare hand catch the floor, and hold the cheek-rest pose. It should look intentionally relaxed, not like a failed spin. The pose needs a readable hold (about 0.75–1.0 second is a useful starting point) and a stable supporting elbow/hip. The source is only four frames per second, so exact transition velocities cannot be recovered from it.

Both floor references finish low. The first is an athletic support freeze; the second is a comic recline. Their useful common sequence is **spin → broaden/brake → place support → settle → hold**, with no intervening stand-up.
